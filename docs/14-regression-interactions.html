14  Regression: interactions and non-linear associations

Everything so far has assumed two things about a regression, and both are often wrong.

The first is that each predictor has the same effect for everybody. The second is that the relationship is a straight line. This session relaxes them one at a time.

library(ggplot2)
library(dplyr)
library(psych)
library(ggeffects)

swe <- readRDS("data/swe.rds")

trust_items <- c(
  "tr_parl", "tr_govt", "tr_court",
  "tr_sci", "tr_party", "tr_media"
)
swe$trust <- fa(swe[, trust_items], nfactors = 1, fm = "ml")$scores[, 1]

swe$sex <- factor(
  swe$female,
  levels = c(0, 1),
  labels = c("Men", "Women")
)

14.1 Interactions

In session 11 we found that trust in institutions predicts how democratic people think Sweden is, and that it does so about equally on both sides of the political divide once you control for it.

But “controlling for” and “the same for everybody” are different claims. A model with trust + bloc says the trust slope is identical for left and right voters, and forces it to be. An interaction lets it differ.

model_int <- lm(demlevel ~ trust * bloc, data = swe)

summary(model_int)

## F-statistic: 314.7 on 3 and 2140 DF,  p-value: < 2.2e-16

trust * bloc is shorthand. It puts in trust, bloc, and the product of the two — the interaction term, printed as trust:blocRight. You could write trust + bloc + trust:bloc and get the same model.

14.1.1 Reading it

Four numbers, and the trap is in the middle two.

(Intercept) = 7.936 — predicted rating for a left-bloc voter with average trust.

trust = 0.955 — the trust slope for left-bloc voters only. Not for everyone. Not on average.

blocRight = -0.134 — the bloc difference at trust = 0. Not overall.

trust:blocRight = 0.211 — how much the trust slope changes for right-bloc voters. So their slope is 0.955 + 0.211 = 1.166.

Once you add an interaction, the other two coefficients change meaning. They are no longer average effects. They are the effect of one variable when the other is zero, which is why it matters enormously whether zero is a meaningful value.

Here it happens to be fine, because trust is a factor score, so zero is the average. Had we used raw age, blocRight would have been the bloc difference among newborns.

The interaction is significant (p = 0.003), so trust does more work for right-bloc voters than for left-bloc voters: 1.166 points per unit of trust against 0.955.

14.1.2 Plot it, always

Interaction coefficients are close to unreadable in a table. Nobody should have to do that arithmetic in their head, and the sign of an interaction term tells you almost nothing on its own.

predictions <- predict_response(
  model_int,
  terms = c("trust [-2:2 by=0.5]", "bloc")
)

plot(predictions) +
  scale_colour_viridis_d(end = 0.7) +
  scale_fill_viridis_d(end = 0.7) +
  labs(
    x = "Trust in institutions (factor score)",
    y = "Predicted rating of Swedish democracy",
    colour = "Bloc",
    fill = "Bloc",
    title = NULL
  ) +
  theme_bw()

Giving terms two variables produces one line per group of the second, and scale_colour_viridis_d() and scale_fill_viridis_d() put the two lines on the colourblind-safe palette from session 4. Now the interaction is obvious: two lines that are not parallel, and that cross.

Read what it says. Among people who distrust institutions, left-bloc voters rate Swedish democracy higher than right-bloc voters. Among people who trust institutions, it is the other way round. The lines meet at a trust score of about 0.63.

So the answer to “is there a winner–loser gap?” is: it depends who you ask. That is what an interaction means, and no single coefficient could have said it.

Non-parallel lines are what an interaction is. If you cannot see it on this plot, it is not there in any meaningful sense, whatever the p-value says.

Note also the top right corner: the model predicts 10.13 for the most trusting right-bloc voters, on a scale that stops at 10. Session 12 again, and one more reason not to read predictions at the edges too literally.

14.1.3 When the main effect misleads

A second example, because this catches people.

model_age <- lm(lr_self ~ age * sex, data = swe)

summary(model_age)

## (Intercept)   5.901548   0.262858  22.451  < 2e-16 ***

## sexWomen     -1.831944   0.359364  -5.098  3.7e-07 ***
## age:sexWomen  0.018891   0.006144   3.075  0.00213 ** 
## ---
## Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1
## 
## Residual standard error: 2.759 on 2444 degrees of freedom
##   (397 observations deleted due to missingness)
## Multiple R-squared:  0.02481,    Adjusted R-squared:  0.02361 
## F-statistic: 20.73 on 3 and 2444 DF,  p-value: 2.926e-13

The coefficient on age is -0.0047 with p = 0.293 — nothing. Read carelessly, that says age does not matter.

It says no such thing. It says age does not matter for men, since men are the reference category. For women the slope is -0.0047 + 0.0189 = 0.0142 — positive, and the interaction is significant at p = 0.002.

age_predictions <- predict_response(model_age, terms = c("age", "sex"))

plot(age_predictions) +
  scale_colour_viridis_d(end = 0.7) +
  scale_fill_viridis_d(end = 0.7) +
  labs(
    x = "Age (years)",
    y = "Predicted left-right self-placement",
    colour = "Sex",
    fill = "Sex",
    title = NULL
  ) +
  theme_bw()

Older women place themselves further right than younger women; among men, age makes little difference. Two groups, two different stories, and a model without the interaction would have averaged them into one weak effect.

A question. In model_age, swapping the reference category from men to women would change the coefficient on age from -0.0047 to 0.0142, and its p-value from non-significant to significant.

Nothing about the data has changed. What does that tell you about reporting “the effect of age” from a model containing an interaction?

Do not go hunting. With ten predictors there are forty-five possible two-way interactions, and at the conventional threshold you would expect a couple to come out significant by chance alone. Test interactions you have a reason to expect. An interaction found by searching needs replicating before it means anything.

14.2 Non-linear relationships

The second assumption. Sometimes there is a reason to expect that the relationship is not a straight line.

Who likes the Liberals? The theory of proximity voting says that people like the parties that are close to them. Respondents see the Liberals as a party a little to the right of the centre: on average they place it at 6.4 on the 0–10 scale. So the rating should rise as we move from the left towards the party, and fall again on the other side of it. Up, and then down.

14.2.1 A straight line

Fit a straight line anyway and see.

linear <- lm(like_l ~ lr_self, data = swe)

14.2.2 A squared term

To let the line bend, add the square of the predictor as a second predictor. The model can then bend once: up and then down, or down and then up. This is called a quadratic model.

\[\text{rating} = b_0 + b_1 \times \text{left-right} + b_2 \times \text{left-right}^2\]

quadratic <- lm(like_l ~ lr_self + I(lr_self^2), data = swe)

summary(quadratic)
## 
## Call:
## lm(formula = like_l ~ lr_self + I(lr_self^2), data = swe)
## 
## Residuals:
##     Min      1Q  Median      3Q     Max 
## -5.5177 -1.4541  0.1064  1.5630  7.0789 
## 
## Coefficients:
##               Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|)    
## (Intercept)   1.921091   0.144601   13.29   <2e-16 ***
## lr_self       1.019095   0.062180   16.39   <2e-16 ***
## I(lr_self^2) -0.072184   0.005966  -12.10   <2e-16 ***
## ---
## Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1
## 
## Residual standard error: 2.271 on 2287 degrees of freedom
##   (555 observations deleted due to missingness)
## Multiple R-squared:  0.1632, Adjusted R-squared:  0.1624 
## F-statistic: 222.9 on 2 and 2287 DF,  p-value: < 2.2e-16

I() is needed around lr_self^2. Inside a formula ^ means something else — interactions to a given order — and I() says “treat this as ordinary arithmetic”.

The squared term is clearly significant, which says that the relationship is not a straight line. Adjusted R² rises from 0.109 to 0.162. The adjusted version is the one to compare, because the second model has one more term.

set.seed(2026)

swe |>
  filter(!is.na(lr_self), !is.na(like_l)) |>
  ggplot(aes(x = lr_self, y = like_l)) +

  geom_smooth(
    method = "lm",
    formula = y ~ x,
    se = FALSE,
    colour = "grey50",
    linetype = "dashed"
  ) +
  geom_smooth(
    method = "lm",
    formula = y ~ x + I(x^2),
    se = FALSE,
    colour = "black"
  ) +
  geom_point(data = lr_means, aes(y = mean), size = 2.5) +
  scale_x_continuous(breaks = 0:10) +
  labs(
    x = "Left-right self-placement (0 = left, 10 = right)",
    y = "Rating of the Liberals (0-10)"
  ) +

The grey points are the respondents and the black ones the mean at each point of the scale. The dashed line is the straight fit and the solid one the quadratic. The straight line is too high at both ends and too low in the middle — the signature of fitting a line to a curve, and exactly what the residual plot in session 11 was looking for.

14.2.3 Reading a quadratic

A squared term is like an interaction: the two coefficients cannot be read separately. “One point further right, holding the square of left-right constant” is not something that can happen. Read them together.

The signs give the shape. The first coefficient is positive (1.02) and the squared one is negative (-0.072): the curve rises, rises less and less, and then falls. Had the signs been the other way round, it would fall first and then rise.

Predictions give the size. There is no single effect of left-right placement any more, so compare one-point steps in different places.

steps <- data.frame(lr_self = c(1, 2, 6, 7, 9, 10))
steps$predicted <- predict(quadratic, newdata = steps)

round(steps, 3)
##   lr_self predicted
## 1       1     2.868
## 2       2     3.671
## 3       6     5.437
## 4       7     5.518
## 5       9     5.246
## 6      10     4.894

From 1 to 2 the predicted rating rises by 0.803. From 6 to 7 it barely moves (0.081). From 9 to 10 it falls by 0.352.

The turning point says where it stops rising. It is minus the first coefficient divided by twice the second.

b <- coef(quadratic)

-b[2] / (2 * b[3])
##  lr_self 
## 7.059006

7.1, not far from where respondents place the party. That is what proximity would lead us to expect.

14.2.4 What to check

Is the turning point inside the data? A quadratic always turns round somewhere, whether or not the world does. If the turning point lies outside the data, or only a handful of cases lie beyond it, the bend downwards is how a quadratic is built and not a finding. Here about 25% of the respondents are beyond it, and their means do fall.

Is the curve close to the data? A quadratic is symmetric around its turning point, and the world need not be. Here the prediction for people at 10 is 4.89, but their actual mean is 4.17. The curve gets the shape right, not every point on it.

Do not go beyond the data. Carried far enough, a quadratic predicts things that cannot exist, exactly like a straight line.

Have a reason for the curve. We had one: people like parties that are close to them. Adding squared terms to see what becomes significant is hunting, just as it is with interactions.

A question. The rating of the Moderates by left-right self-placement is close to a straight line. The rating of the Liberals is not.

If people like parties that are close to them, why should the two look so different?

How to write it up.

An interaction:

The association between trust and evaluations of democracy differed by bloc (b = 0.21, SE = 0.07, p = 0.003). Among left-bloc voters a one-unit increase in the trust score corresponded to a 0.95-point higher rating; among right-bloc voters, 1.17 points (Figure 1).

A curve:

The rating of the Liberals was modelled as a quadratic function of left-right self-placement, since people are expected to like parties that are close to them. The squared term was negative and significant (b = -0.072, SE = 0.006, p < .001): the rating rose from the left, peaked at about 7.1 on the 0-10 scale and fell towards the right (Figure 3; adjusted R² = 0.16, n = 2290).

Mistakes to avoid:

  • Reporting an interaction without a plot. The coefficients are not readable on their own.
  • Interpreting a main effect as an average effect when an interaction is in the model. It is the effect when the other variable is zero.
  • Reporting a main effect’s p-value from a model with an interaction, as though it meant something general. It depends on the reference category.
  • Fishing for interactions. Test the ones you predicted.
  • Adding a squared term only because it raises R². The shape should follow from what the variables are.
  • Reading the two coefficients of a quadratic separately. Give predicted values, or a plot.
  • Not checking where the turning point is. It may lie outside the data, or rest on a handful of cases.
  • Extrapolating a quadratic, which always turns round in the end.
